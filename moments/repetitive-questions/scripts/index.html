She Asks the Same Question Every 2 Minutes: The Written-Answer Trick + Script

You've answered kindly nineteen times. The twentieth is forming in your throat with an edge on it. The problem isn't your patience — it's that a spoken answer evaporates. A written one stays in the room.

Why writing works when your voice can't

As our main guide on repetitive questions explains, the loop is usually anxiety looking for comfort, security, and familiarity — the Alzheimer's Association's own framing — landing on a brain that can't hold your answer. A card they can re-read gives them the reassurance without needing you as the delivery mechanism. Each glance re-answers the question identically, patiently, at 2 a.m. as willingly as at noon. It works while the person can still read and make sense of short text — which is often true well into the middle stage.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different. A question loop that starts suddenly, or comes with new confusion or sleepiness, is medical until a clinician says otherwise.

Setting up the card (5 minutes, once)

  1. One question per card. The big loop only — "When is Mary coming?" gets its own card. Two questions on one card is a document; nobody re-reads a document in a worried moment.
  2. Short answer, warm words, big print. "Mary is coming at 3 o'clock. She loves visiting you." Dark marker on white card, letters at least an inch tall, nothing else on the page.
  3. Put it where the question happens. Their chair, the kitchen table, beside the bed — the place they are when the worry starts. A card in a drawer answers nothing.
  4. Update it the moment plans change. A card that's wrong once gets distrusted forever. If Mary moves to 4, the card moves to 4 before the question is asked.

Say this (the introduction script)

I wrote it down for us, right here. Let's read it together.
When you wonder, your answer is right here on your card.
It's okay to check as many times as you like. That's what it's for.

The first few times, read it with them, pointing at the words — you're teaching the route, not just the fact. After that, when the question comes, answer once out loud and gently gesture: "And it's right there on your card too." Over days, many people start glancing at the card before they ask.

Don't say this

"Read your card! I made it so you'd stop asking me."

The card is a comfort object, not a reprimand. The moment it's waved at them in frustration, it stops meaning "you're safe" and starts meaning "you're a burden" — and the anxiety underneath the question gets worse, not better. Also skip quizzing them on it ("What does your card say?") — testing memory is the trap this whole page exists to avoid.

The 60-second step, when the loop is running hot

  1. Answer the feeling first (15 seconds): "You sound worried. I'm here."
  2. Read the card together (20 seconds): out loud, your finger under the words, unhurried.
  3. Bridge out (25 seconds): "While we wait for Mary — will you help me with these?" Give attention a job.

When reading starts to fade

  • Shorten the card: one line, bigger letters. "MARY — 3 O'CLOCK" can outlast a paragraph by months.
  • Add a picture: Mary's photo beside the words carries the reassurance when text stops landing.
  • Keep the ritual, change the medium: the same identical spoken answer, in the same calm words, becomes the card. Sameness is the active ingredient — it always was.
