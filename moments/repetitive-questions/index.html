Never Say "Don't You Remember?": 5 Phrases to Delete, 5 to Use Instead

"When is Mary coming?" Forty times since breakfast. Your answer isn't failing — it was never really about the answer. Here's what the question usually means, and the words that actually reach it.

The question is a feeling in disguise

Short-term memory loss means your answer genuinely doesn't stay. But the Alzheimer's Association points to the deeper driver: repetition is usually a person "looking for comfort, security and familiarity." The question is the anxiety wearing a sentence. "When is Mary coming?" often means I'm unsettled and I need to know I'm not alone. "When's lunch?" can mean hungry, bored, in discomfort, or needing the toilet. Answer the feeling first and the factual question second, and the loop often loosens — answer only the facts, in exactly the same tone of rising irritation, and it tightens.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different.

Delete these five

"Don't you remember?"
"I just told you that!"
"What did I just say? Go on — tell me."
"We've been through this."
"You asked me that five minutes ago."

Every one of these tests a memory that the disease has taken, and the test always ends the same way: shame for them, guilt for you, and the question back again in two minutes — now with more anxiety attached. The National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Society are explicit: don't ask questions just to test memory. It produces nothing but distress, and the answer still won't be retained.

Use these five instead

"Mary is coming at three. You're looking forward to seeing her."
"Lunch is at twelve. Are you hungry now? Let's have a little something."
"I'm right here. We're at home, and everything's taken care of."
"You sound worried. Tell me what's on your mind."
"While we wait — will you help me fold these?"

The pattern: a short, identical factual answer, the feeling named kindly, then a gentle bridge into something absorbing. Answer in the same words each time — identical wording is less new information to process, and the sameness itself becomes reassuring.

The 60-second step

  1. Answer the feeling (15 seconds): name what might be underneath — worry, hunger, boredom, the toilet — before the fact.
  2. Give the short answer, same words as last time (15 seconds): no preamble, no sigh they can hear.
  3. Bridge to an activity (30 seconds): a task, a snack, a song, a walk to the window. Attention needs somewhere to go.

What helps over time

  • The written answer: if your person can still read, the answer on a card or whiteboard where they can see it answers the question forty times without your voice wearing out. This is the single most practical trick for the biggest loops — we walk through it in the written-answer guide.
  • Check the body basics on a schedule: toilet, drink, snack, pain — offered before the asking starts.
  • Protect your own tone: they will forget your words within minutes and remember your irritation for hours. If you feel the snap coming, that's a signal you need a break and support — not a character flaw. Caregiver helplines are on our support page.

By the thirtieth time, nobody's voice stays kind on its own.
