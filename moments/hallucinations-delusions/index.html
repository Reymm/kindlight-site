Hallucinations in Dementia: What to Say and What Never to Say

"There are children in the bedroom." There aren't. Every instinct says to correct it — and correction is the one move that reliably makes it worse. The question that matters isn't "is it real?" It's "is she frightened?"

What's happening

A hallucination is experiencing something that isn't there — most often seeing people, children, or animals. A delusion is a firmly held false belief, like theft (covered in our stealing guide), infidelity, or that a spouse is an imposter. Both arise from the disease's effect on perception and interpretation — and both can also be triggered or worsened by things outside the dementia: infection, dehydration, pain, poor vision or hearing, and medicines. That second list is why new hallucinations are a medical question first, as the banner below explains.

Your response hinges on one assessment: fear, not content. A calm person chatting with visitors only they can see needs a different response from a terrified person hiding from intruders.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different. New hallucinations, or a clear step-up in how often they happen, always deserve a same-day call to the doctor — infection and medication effects are common, treatable causes.

One exception to the urgency: in Lewy body dementia, vivid hallucinations are a core, expected feature and are often not frightening at all. They are not, by themselves, an emergency — but they come with a critical medication warning. Read the Lewy body guide if that's your person's diagnosis.

Say this

I don't see the children, but I can see you're worried. I'm here — you're safe.
That sounds frightening. Let's sit together until it feels calmer.
Let's go into the other room and put some music on.

Honest about your own perception, anchored on their feeling, and ending in company and a change of scene. If the experience isn't frightening and isn't driving unsafe behaviour, you often don't need to challenge it at all — acknowledge, stay close, and let the moment pass or gently redirect it.

Don't say this

"There's nobody there — you're imagining it."

You're arguing with a perception. To them it is exactly as real as you are, so the argument lands as "you're lying to me" or "you can't see the danger" — fear goes up, trust goes down. The opposite error is just as unhelpful: elaborately building on a frightening belief ("Yes, the men are outside, we'd better hide") constructs a world that gets scarier. And never restrain someone who is acting on a belief — make the environment safe and redirect instead.

The 60-second step

  1. Assess fear, not content (10 seconds): calm or frightened? Your whole response follows from that.
  2. Reassure and stay (25 seconds): eye level, their name, "I'm here — you're safe." If you gently offer your own perception once and it's rejected, drop it.
  3. Change the inputs (25 seconds): lights up, glasses and hearing aids on, TV off if it might be feeding it, and move rooms with music or a drink. Then note the time and what was happening — that log is gold for their doctor.

What helps over time

  • Fix the triggers you can: dim rooms, patterned curtains, mirrors, and certain TV programmes are repeat offenders. Better lighting and current glasses/hearing-aid prescriptions genuinely reduce episodes for many people.
  • Keep a simple log: when, what they perceived, how frightened they were, what helped. Bring it to appointments — patterns point clinicians to causes.
  • Acting on a belief unsafely — trying to "rescue" someone, fleeing the house, confronting a "stranger" who is actually family — is a safety problem, not a conversation problem. Make the environment safe, stay with them, and treat it as a same-day conversation with their care team.

You don't have to find these words while she's frightened.
