Sundowning: A 5 PM Checklist That Actually Helps

Late afternoon arrives, and so does the restlessness — pacing, suspicion, tears, the repeated question, the demand to go home. Sundowning is one of the most predictable moments in dementia care, which means you can get ahead of it.

What's underneath it

Sundowning — increased confusion and agitation from late afternoon into the night — isn't fully understood, but the contributors are consistent across the Alzheimer's Association and National Institute on Aging guidance: a disrupted internal body clock, plain end-of-day fatigue, fading light and shadows that get misread as strangers, hunger, pain, constipation, a broken night's sleep, or too much stimulation earlier in the day. Think of it as an unmet-need pile-up landing at the hour when your person has the least left to cope with. You can't remove the hour. You can change what's in it.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different. Sundowning that is new, sharply worse, or comes with fever, new sleepiness, or not eating or drinking needs a same-day medical review.

The checklist: the hour before it usually starts

  1. Lights up before dusk. Turn lamps on while it's still light outside, then close the curtains. A bright, even-lit room removes the shadows that get misread as people.
  2. Noise down. TV off or to something calm and familiar. One voice at a time. No difficult phone calls, visitors arriving, or tasks starting in this window.
  3. Body basics covered. Toilet trip, a drink, a snack. A surprising share of 5 p.m. agitation is a full bladder and an empty stomach.
  4. Hands and mouth busy. Folding towels, sorting buttons, a familiar song on, something warm to drink. An occupied person sundowns less.
  5. Same words, same order. A short, predictable evening sequence — tea, programme, pyjamas — repeated daily becomes a handrail they can hold.

Say this (and repeat the same line)

You're safe. I'm here with you.
Let's sit together and have something warm to drink.
It's evening now. We'll stay here together tonight.

Repeat the same reassurance rather than finding new wording. New sentences are new information to process; the familiar line becomes the comfort itself.

Don't say this

"There's nothing there — look, the room is empty."

Arguing about what's real, or reasoning at length, asks a tired brain to do the one thing it can't do right now. And don't darken the room early "to help them sleep" — shadows grow, misperception grows with them, and the evening gets worse, not better.

The 60-second step, when it's already happening

  1. Get lower and slower (10 seconds): sit down near them, match their pace, soften your voice.
  2. One line, repeated (20 seconds): "You're safe. I'm here with you." Say it calmly, more than once.
  3. Move the moment (30 seconds): offer a warm drink or a small task with your hands together — carry the moment somewhere softer.

What helps over time (the daytime half of the fix)

  • Morning daylight: bright light early in the day helps anchor the body clock that slips by evening.
  • Front-load the day: activity, outings, and anything demanding in the morning; a big lunch and a lighter evening meal.
  • Guard the nap: long late-afternoon naps borrow from the night and add to evening confusion. Caffeine stays a morning thing.

A note on Lewy body dementia

In Lewy body dementia, attention and alertness naturally fluctuate — a clear hour can be followed by a drowsy, confused one without anything being "wrong." Expect the swing, plan demanding things for the clearer windows, and don't judge what they can do by their best hour. A sustained step down, though, still deserves a same-day medical check.
