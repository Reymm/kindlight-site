He Won't Eat: Appetite Loss in Dementia — Comfort-First Steps

A full plate, untouched. Or a fork held like a riddle. When someone with dementia won't eat, the causes are usually practical and fixable — and the pressure to "just eat something" is the one ingredient that never helps.

Why eating breaks down

Work through the list the way clinicians do. They may no longer recognise the food — or the utensils — as what they are. A full, varied plate can overwhelm a brain that handles one thing at a time. The sequence itself can be lost: they may not know how to start. Add mouth or dental pain, constipation, low mood, changed taste (a sweet preference is common), medication effects, or simple forgetfulness about whether they've eaten. And in later stages, swallowing itself becomes difficult — which changes this from a persuasion problem into a safety one, covered below.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different.

Call the same day if: food and fluids are refused for more than 24 hours, weight is dropping quickly, or there are signs of dehydration. Get prompt help for swallowing: coughing or choking on food or drink, food held in the cheek, or a wet, gurgly voice after swallowing means stop, keep them upright, and arrange a doctor visit and a swallowing (speech-language) assessment. Choking with difficulty breathing is a 911 call.

Say this

I'm having some of this chicken — let's enjoy it together.
Try just one bite with me.
Here's your tea. Hold it with both hands, like this.

Sitting down and eating with them is the strongest cue there is — modelling beats instructing. "One bite with me" shrinks the task to something startable.

Don't say this

"You have to eat or you'll get sick."

Threats and health reasoning don't survive contact with a brain that can't weigh consequences — they just add fear to the table. The other trap is the banquet: a large multi-item meal plus "What do you want?" is choice overload stacked on recognition problems. And never push food or fluids into the mouth of someone who isn't cooperating — that's an aspiration risk, not a feeding technique.

The 60-second step

  1. Shrink the plate (20 seconds): one or two familiar foods, on a plain plate in a colour that contrasts with the food, on an uncluttered table.
  2. Start the motion (20 seconds): sit, eat a bite yourself, then guide their hand under yours to begin the first bite. Muscle memory often takes it from there.
  3. Switch format if it's stuck (20 seconds): finger foods if cutlery is the barrier. Small, frequent, nutrient-dense offers beat three large negotiations.

What helps over time

  • Drinks, repeatedly, in the right cup: a cup they recognise and can grip, offered often rather than in large amounts. Fluid refusal becomes dehydration faster than food refusal becomes anything.
  • Check the mouth: denture fit, sores, and dental pain are silent appetite-killers — worth a dental or doctor visit when eating drops off.
  • Keep a simple intake note: what was eaten and drunk, roughly. If you do need to call the doctor, "two small meals and one glass of water since yesterday" gets you a very different conversation than "he won't eat."

A note on later stages

Late in the disease, eating support becomes comfort care: favourite tastes, mouth comfort, unhurried presence — not intake targets. If your person is in that stage, the goal of this page changes from "how do I get enough in" to "how do I keep this pleasant and safe," and their care team should be part of that conversation.
