Refusing Medication in Dementia: What You Can Do — and What You Must Never Do

The pills are in your palm. Her mouth is closed, or the tablet comes straight back out. This page has two halves: a calm approach that works more often than force ever will — and a short, absolute list of things that are never safe to improvise.

Why the refusal happens

From their side, the pill may be unrecognisable — they don't remember being prescribed it, or what it's for. The taste may be unpleasant, swallowing may have become harder, the medicine may make them feel nauseous or dizzy, a row of tablets can feel overwhelming, or suspicion may be doing the talking ("you're poisoning me" belongs to the same family as our stealing-accusation guide). Feeling controlled is a trigger all by itself. None of that makes the medicine optional — it makes the approach the variable you can actually change.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different.

If you think too much medicine may have been taken — by anyone — call Poison Help (US) at 1-800-222-1222 right away, or 911 if they seem unwell.

Say this

It's time for your heart pill. It helps keep you strong.
Would you like it with water or with juice?
What feels difficult about taking this one? We can ask the pharmacist together.

Stay matter-of-fact. Present one pill at a time, with a drink — or with a food the pharmacist or prescriber has already said is fine for that medicine. Give the purpose in their terms, briefly. A small choice about timing or drink hands back a little control, which is often what the refusal was protecting.

What you must never do

This list has no exceptions, and it's the most important part of this page:

  • Never crush, split, or dissolve a medicine to make it easier to give — changing the form can change how the drug works and can be dangerous. Only the pharmacist or prescriber can say whether a form can be altered.
  • Never hide medicine in food or drink without the prescriber's and pharmacist's explicit guidance.
  • Never double up a later dose to "make up" for a refused one, and never guess whether a missed dose should be taken late — ask the pharmacist or prescriber. Do not assume delaying is safe, either.
  • Never skip, stop, swap, or substitute a medicine on your own judgement — and never give one person's medicine to another.
  • Never force or threaten ("If you don't take it, I'll have to call the doctor"). Force destroys trust and makes the next dose harder.

These aren't our rules — they reflect the Alzheimer's Association's medication-safety guidance, and they exist because well-meaning improvisation around medication is where real harm happens in home care.

The 60-second step

  1. Stay calm and shrink the moment (15 seconds): one pill, one drink, one short sentence about what it's for.
  2. Offer the small choice (20 seconds): water or juice, now or in a few minutes — choices already within what the prescriber has approved.
  3. If it's still no, pause (25 seconds): don't force it. Note exactly which dose was refused, and try once more a little later. Then let the pharmacist or prescriber — not improvisation — decide what happens next.

When to call the same day

  • A refused medicine is one their clinician has flagged as critical (for example insulin, heart, or anti-seizure medicines — as flagged by their care team).
  • You suspect a dose was taken twice, taken by the wrong person, or taken in the wrong amount.
  • They're coughing on pills, or swallowing seems difficult — that's a swallowing assessment conversation, promptly.
  • Refusal is a new pattern, not a one-off. Bring the log of missed doses to the pharmacist or prescriber — patterns are information they can act on.
