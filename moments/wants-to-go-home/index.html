What to Say When a Parent With Dementia Says "I Want to Go Home"

She is home. She's standing in her own kitchen. And she's asking — sometimes pleading — to go home. Here's what she usually means, and what to say.

What "home" usually means

When a person with dementia says "I want to go home," they are rarely asking for an address. "Home" is usually the feeling of being safe, settled, and surrounded by the familiar — often the home of their childhood or early adult life, the one their memory still holds clearly. The Alzheimer's Association describes behaviour like this as communication: an alarm signalling an unmet need underneath. Before anything else, run the quiet checklist — do they need the toilet? Are they hungry, thirsty, tired, in pain, or unsettled by visitors, noise, or the evening light changing? Very often the request fades once the real need is met.

Arguing the geography never works, because the request was never about geography. The feeling is real even when the facts aren't. So answer the feeling first.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different.

Say this

You want to go home. Tell me about home — what did your kitchen look like?
You're safe here. I'm right here with you.
Let's have a cup of tea together first, then we'll decide.

Asking about the home they mean invites reminiscing, which is often the comfort they were actually looking for. Keep sentences short, use their name, and give them time — a good ten seconds — to answer.

Don't say this

"You live here. This IS your home."

Correction tells them they're wrong about the thing they feel most sure of, and leaves the feeling underneath completely untouched. And if they're asking for a parent who died years ago, "Your mother died years ago" delivers that bereavement as fresh news, every single time. Neither argument nor grief will settle the moment — comfort will.

The 60-second step

  1. Check the basics (15 seconds): toilet, drink, pain, tiredness. Offer the toilet and a drink without making it a quiz.
  2. Validate and ask (20 seconds): "You want to go home. Tell me about it." Let them describe it. Listen like it's the first time.
  3. Reassure and redirect (25 seconds): "You're safe, I'm here." Then move gently toward something warm and absorbing — tea, a familiar song, folding towels, a short walk together that loops back inside.

What helps over time

  • Anchor the room: familiar photos, their own chair, a well-worn blanket. The more the space matches the "home" in their memory, the less often the alarm sounds.
  • Keep a gentle routine: predictable days lower the background anxiety that turns into "I need to go home" by evening.
  • Notice the pattern: if it comes at the same hour daily, it may be tiredness or sundowning — see our sundowning checklist for getting ahead of it.

A note on stages

Early on, if your person asks directly where they live and can take in the answer, a gentle factual answer is fine. In the middle and later stages, correcting identity and home facts almost always backfires — validate the feeling and redirect instead. The advice hasn't changed because the disease has progressed; the words land differently.

If they move from asking to leaving — heading for the door, or found outside — that is a safety situation, not a conversation. If they are missing and not found within about 15 minutes, or are out in dangerous weather or traffic, call 911 and say a person with dementia is missing.
