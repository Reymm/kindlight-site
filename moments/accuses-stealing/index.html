"You Stole My Money": Theft Accusations in Dementia, Explained Gently

Of everything dementia does, this one lands hardest: the person you cook for, bathe, and sit up with at night looks you in the eye and accuses you of stealing from them. Here's what's actually happening in their brain — and why the response that feels natural makes it worse.

How a missing purse becomes an accusation

Walk the sequence from inside their memory loss. They put the purse somewhere safe — and the memory of doing it never formed. They look where it "should" be. It's gone. Their wallet is emptier than they expect, because the spending isn't remembered either. A brain that can't find an explanation doesn't leave a blank: it fills the gap with the most available story, and "someone took it" is a story that makes the world make sense again. The Alzheimer's Association and Alzheimer's Society describe exactly this pattern — suspicion and false beliefs driven by memory loss. The belief is false. To them, it is completely, frighteningly real.

That last part is the whole guide: you are not arguing with a liar or a cruel streak. You're answering a frightened person who believes they've been robbed — often by the only person close enough to be cast in the role.

First, a safety check. If this behaviour is new or suddenly much worse in the last hours or days, that can be a medical emergency (delirium — often from infection, dehydration, pain, or a new medicine). Contact their doctor or urgent care the same day. Dementia changes slowly; sudden changes are different. Suspicion that arrives suddenly, over days rather than months, deserves a same-day call to the doctor.

Say this

Your purse is missing? That's frightening. Let's find it together.
I can see you're worried. I'll help you look.
Let's have a drink while we think where else to look.

Respond to the fear, not the charge. Then join the search — check their usual hiding places together: coat pockets, under cushions, the "safe" drawer, the car. Finding the item side by side closes the episode in a way no verdict ever will.

Don't say this

"I didn't take it! Why would I steal from you?"

Defending yourself puts you on trial in a courtroom where the evidence is their certainty. Long factual defences and logic escalate the suspicion — if you're arguing this hard, the frightened brain concludes, you must have something to hide. Skip the trial. Go straight to the search.

The 60-second step

  1. Name the feeling (10 seconds): "That's frightening. Let's find it together."
  2. Start looking, together (35 seconds): check the top two or three usual spots, narrating kindly as you go.
  3. Close gently (15 seconds): found or not, redirect — a drink, a sit-down, a change of room. If it's not found, promise to keep looking after tea, and mean it.

What helps over time

  • Duplicates of the treasures: a spare pair of glasses, a second wallet with a little cash in it, a copy of the beloved handbag. A found duplicate ends the panic while the original stays "missing."
  • One fixed home for keys and wallet — the same bowl, the same hook, every time — so "where it should be" is a real place.
  • Tell family early, and keep simple records. These accusations often land on the main caregiver and can split families who hear them secondhand. A quiet note of what was spent, and siblings who understand the pattern before it points at them, protect everyone — including you.

One important exception

Most theft accusations in dementia follow the pattern above. But the Alzheimer's Society also cautions that a disclosure can sometimes be true — people with dementia are, sadly, at higher risk of real financial exploitation. If an accusation comes with new specifics — a name, an injury, fear of a particular person, unexplained withdrawals — don't file it under "the dementia." In the US, the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) can route you to Adult Protective Services; in Canada, dial 2-1-1 for local seniors' safety services. You don't have to prove anything to ask for help.

The accusation hurts. The response doesn't have to.
